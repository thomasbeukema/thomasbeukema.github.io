For years, the division of labour in software development was clear. Product Owners and business analysts decided *what* needed to be built and *why*; engineers decided *how* to realise it technically. As long as you had deep technical knowledge and wrote clean, scalable code, your value was undisputed.

In the age of generative AI and autonomous coding agents, that foundation is shifting under our feet. As writing code becomes more of a commodity — something agents can generate, refactor and test in seconds — scarcity in an engineering organisation moves radically. The limiting factor is no longer how fast we can build software, but whether we are building the right things. That makes a product mindset no longer an exclusive skill for Product Managers, but a survival condition for every engineer.

## Code is no longer the bottleneck — context and direction are

The promise of AI-assisted development is seductive: higher speed, less boilerplate, shorter lead times. But anyone who leads a larger cloud platform or application landscape knows that pure execution speed without direction is dangerous.

Give autonomous agents or AI copilots to a team that works in a purely ticket-driven way — *build exactly what is in this Jira ticket without asking questions* — and you do not get value creation. You only produce software nobody is waiting for faster, or you accumulate complex technical debt at record speed.

When the technical barrier to building something drops, the value of being critical *before* a single line of code is generated rises. Which user or platform problem are we actually solving? Does the added complexity in our architecture outweigh the real impact? And can we solve this problem without adding any new code at all?

Those questions no longer belong only to the Product Owner. They belong to every engineer who takes responsibility for what eventually reaches production.

## From implementing to curating and validating

In a traditional setting, an engineer is often valued for craftsmanship in implementation. In an AI-driven team, that craftsmanship shifts to three other dimensions.

The first is problem definition and domain understanding. AI can build an excellent solution to a poorly understood problem. Engineers who grasp the domain, the customer and the business logic can give their tooling and agents the right frames and constraints. Without that sharpness, the model fills in the gaps itself — and rarely with the trade-offs you would have made.

The second is making sharp trade-offs. When generating three different architecture or feature variants barely costs extra development time, the ability to choose based on user value, cost (FinOps) and maintainability becomes the real core competence. Building has become cheap; choosing remains expensive.

The third is steering on outcome instead of output. In a world where pull requests and commits multiply through AI, output no longer says anything about progress. An engineer with a product mindset looks beyond the merge: is the feature being used, is friction for other teams decreasing, and is stability or conversion improving?

## What this means for internal platforms

This shift applies just as much to teams building internal cloud platforms and infrastructure. There too, AI takes over a large share of the standard work: writing Terraform modules, CI/CD pipelines or migration scripts becomes many times faster.

That is exactly why the excuse to run platform work as an executing ticket factory disappears. Platform engineers need to approach their own work as a product for internal developers: continuously investigating where the real friction in the developer experience sits, which guardrails actually accelerate teams, and where standardisation adds value — and where it holds them back.

A platform without product instinct builds more tooling, faster. A platform with product instinct builds less, but precisely what helps teams move forward.

## The new bar for technical talent

The line between "the business" and "IT" was always artificial, but in the age of AI it becomes definitively untenable. Engineers who keep hiding behind purely technical specifications and wait for someone else to chew the problem for them risk being overtaken by the speed of the technology itself.

The most valuable engineers of the coming years will not be those who can type code the fastest or know the newest AI prompts. They will be the engineers who combine technical depth with a sharp product instinct — people who do not only ask *how* we build something, but especially dare to keep asking *why*.
